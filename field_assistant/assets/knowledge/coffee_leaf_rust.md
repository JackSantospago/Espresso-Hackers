Coffee leaf rust: what it is, how to recognise it, how it spreads and what the farmer can do.

Coffee leaf rust is caused by the fungus Hemileia vastatrix. It only lives on coffee plants. It attacks both arabica and robusta coffee and is one of the most damaging coffee diseases in the world. It was first reported in Kenya in 1861 and has since spread through Asia, Africa and, from 1970, the Americas. (Source: Wikipedia; Pacific Pests, Pathogens & Weeds)

Coffee leaf rust symptoms: look at the underside of the leaves. The disease starts as small pale yellow spots. These grow into round yellow-orange powdery patches, a few millimetres up to about 1.5 cm wide. The orange powder is millions of fungus spores. On the upper side of the leaf you see yellow or later brown spots with a yellow edge. (Source: Wikipedia; Pacific Pests, Pathogens & Weeds)

Coffee leaf rust damage: badly infected leaves fall off early. Trees with heavy crops suffer most, because the tree feeds the berries instead of the leaves, and branches can die back. The loss of leaves reduces the harvest in the following season. Yield losses between 15% and 80% have been reported, depending on the place and year. (Source: Wikipedia; Pacific Pests, Pathogens & Weeds)

Coffee leaf rust spread: the spores are carried by wind, by rain splash, and on hands, clothes and tools of people working in the field. A spore needs free water on the leaf (rain or dew) to infect. Infection is favoured by temperatures between about 16 and 28 °C, with the best germination near 21 °C. One rust spot can release hundreds of thousands of spores over several months. (Source: Wikipedia)

Coffee leaf rust and altitude: the disease is usually worse in warm, wet, lower-altitude coffee areas and in the rainy season. At very high altitudes it is less of a problem; a Pacific fact sheet notes that fungicide sprays are usually not needed above about 1,700 m. (Source: Pacific Pests, Pathogens & Weeds)

Coffee leaf rust management, part 1: the best long-term protection is planting resistant varieties when you replant. In Kenya, Batian and Ruiru 11 are resistant to both leaf rust and coffee berry disease. Catimor types are also resistant. Ask your extension officer or cooperative which resistant seedlings are available in your area. (Source: KALRO; Wikipedia; Pacific Pests, Pathogens & Weeds)

Coffee leaf rust management, part 2: prune so that air moves through the bush and leaves dry quickly after rain. Keep good spacing between trees. Feed the trees well, especially with nitrogen and potassium from manure, compost or fertiliser, because weak and over-cropped trees suffer most. Keep shade moderate, not too dense. (Source: Wikipedia; Pacific Pests, Pathogens & Weeds; Naturland)

Coffee leaf rust and fungicides: copper fungicides protect leaves before infection and are sprayed as a preventive; systemic fungicides (triazoles) can stop new infections. Which product is allowed, how much to use and when to spray must be agreed with your extension officer or cooperative. Do not spray without advice. (Source: Wikipedia; Pacific Pests, Pathogens & Weeds)
