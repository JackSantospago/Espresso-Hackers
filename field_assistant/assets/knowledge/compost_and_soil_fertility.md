Compost, manure and soil fertility: feeding the soil.

Soil fertility basics: crops take nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P) and potassium (K) from the soil at every harvest. If these are not returned with manure, compost, crop residues, legumes or fertiliser, yields fall over the years. (Source: Wikipedia; Naturland)

Making compost: mix green materials (fresh leaves, grass, kitchen scraps, manure), which are rich in nitrogen, with brown materials (dry leaves, straw, maize stalks), which are rich in carbon. Use more brown than green material: the best mix has about 25 to 30 parts carbon to 1 part nitrogen, and too much green material makes the heap smell of ammonia. Keep the heap as moist as a squeezed-out sponge and turn it regularly to let air in. (Source: Wikipedia)

Compost heat and time: a well-built, large heap heats up to about 55 to 70 °C, and can be ready in a few weeks to months. Small, cold heaps can take up to a year. Finished compost is dark brown, crumbly and smells like earth. (Source: Wikipedia)

What not to compost: do not add diseased plants, meat or bones. Diseased leaves can carry fungi back to the field; burn or bury them instead. (Source: Wikipedia)

Using compost and manure: dig compost or well-rotted manure into planting holes or rows, or spread it as mulch. It feeds the crop, improves soil structure, helps sandy soils hold water, and supports helpful soil organisms. (Source: Wikipedia)

Legumes for fertility: beans, cowpeas, groundnuts, soybeans and plants such as Desmodium live with rhizobia bacteria in root nodules that capture nitrogen from the air. When the roots and leaves rot, this nitrogen feeds the next crop. (Source: Wikipedia)
