Coffee leaf miner: what it is, how to recognise it, why outbreaks happen and what the farmer can do.

Coffee leaf miner is a tiny moth whose caterpillars live inside coffee leaves. Several Leucoptera species occur in Africa, including Leucoptera meyricki in East Africa. The adult moth is only about 2 mm long. The female lays tiny eggs on the leaves, and the young caterpillars tunnel into the leaf. (Source: USDA ARS; Plantix)

Coffee leaf miner symptoms: look for irregular brown patches (mines) on the upper side of the leaf, often with pale yellow trails under the leaf skin at the edges. If you hold the leaf against the light or peel the skin, you may see the small caterpillars or their droppings inside. Old mines become large dead brown patches. Silk cocoons are often found on the underside of leaves. (Source: Plantix; USDA ARS)

Coffee leaf miner damage: the caterpillars eat the green tissue inside the leaf, so the leaf can make less food. Badly attacked leaves fall off, and yields drop. Losses of up to 40 to 50% have been recorded in Brazil and Puerto Rico. (Source: USDA ARS)

Coffee leaf miner outbreaks: the pest does best in dry seasons and hot weather and can have 7 to 12 generations a year. Rain kills many caterpillars. Outbreaks often follow heavy insecticide spraying, because the sprays also kill the small wasps and other natural enemies that normally keep leaf miners low. (Source: USDA ARS; Plantix)

Coffee leaf miner management: protect natural enemies. Many parasitic wasps and predatory wasps attack leaf miners, so avoid unnecessary insecticide sprays. Keep good shade and balanced fertilisation, which make the bush less attractive. Monitor the first generation of the season; acting early is most effective. Ask your extension officer before using any insecticide, because leaf miners can become resistant. (Source: USDA ARS; Plantix)
