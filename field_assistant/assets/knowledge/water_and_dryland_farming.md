Water and dryland farming: water harvesting, mulching and conservation agriculture.

Zai planting pits: in dry areas, dig pits about 15 to 50 cm wide and 5 to 15 cm deep, about 80 cm apart, and fill them with manure or compost before planting. The pits catch rainwater and concentrate nutrients around the crop. Zai pits are used across the Sahel and in dry parts of Kenya, and have greatly increased sorghum and millet yields on degraded land. (Source: Wikipedia)

Mulching: covering the soil with straw, crop residue or cut grass reduces evaporation, keeps the soil cooler, protects it from heavy rain and erosion, and adds organic matter as it rots. (Source: Wikipedia)

Conservation agriculture: FAO describes three principles: disturb the soil as little as possible (minimum or no tillage), keep the soil covered with mulch or cover crops, and rotate different crops. This reduces erosion, improves water infiltration and can cut labour. Benefits take a few seasons to appear, and residues used as mulch are no longer available as animal feed. (Source: Wikipedia)

Critical water periods: maize is most sensitive to drought at tasselling and silking; sweet potato about 50 to 60 days after planting. Plant at the right time so these stages fall in the wettest part of the season. (Source: Wikipedia; PlantVillage)

Saving water in rice: alternate wetting and drying lets the paddy water drop below the surface before flooding again and uses less water than continuous flooding. (Source: Wikipedia)
