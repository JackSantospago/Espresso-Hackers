Coffee berry borer: what it is, how to recognise it and what the farmer can do.

Coffee berry borer (Hypothenemus hampei) is a tiny black beetle, about 1.5 mm long, that is native to Africa and now found in most coffee countries. The female bores into the coffee berry and lays her eggs inside, and the young feed on the bean. It is a major pest that affects millions of coffee-farming families. (Source: Wikipedia; USDA ARS)

Coffee berry borer symptoms: look for a small round hole, usually at the tip (the small disc at the end) of green or ripe berries. Cut the berry open: you may find the beetle, larvae and tunnels in the bean. Damaged beans weigh less and have poor quality, which lowers the price. Badly attacked berries can fall early. (Source: Wikipedia; USDA ARS)

Coffee berry borer life cycle: one female lays 35 to 50 eggs, and the young develop into adults in about 24 to 45 days, so several generations can grow on one plant in a season. The beetles survive between seasons in berries left on trees or fallen on the ground. They prefer shady, moist spots. (Source: Wikipedia)

Coffee berry borer management: the most important step is clean harvesting. Pick all ripe and overripe berries, collect berries that fell on the ground, and remove all leftover berries from the trees at the end of the harvest (strip picking), so the beetles have nowhere to breed. The fungus Beauveria bassiana and small parasitic wasps are used as biological control. Insecticides work poorly because the beetle hides inside the berry. (Source: Wikipedia; USDA ARS; Naturland)
