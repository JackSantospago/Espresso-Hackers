Fall armyworm in maize: how to recognise it and what the farmer can do.

Fall armyworm (Spodoptera frugiperda) is a moth whose caterpillars eat maize and many other crops. It came from the Americas, arrived in Africa in 2016 and spread quickly across the continent. It can destroy a field very fast because older caterpillars eat about 50 times more than young ones. (Source: Wikipedia)

Fall armyworm identification: mature caterpillars are about 4 to 5 cm long and brown or greenish. Look for a pale upside-down Y mark on the head and four black dots arranged in a square on the second-last body segment. Eggs are laid in clusters on leaves and covered with whitish, cotton-like hairs. (Source: KALRO; Pacific Pests, Pathogens & Weeds)

Fall armyworm damage on maize: young caterpillars scrape the leaf and leave see-through window-pane patches. Older caterpillars make large ragged holes, and the leaves look torn. Wet sawdust-like droppings (frass) collect in the funnel (whorl) of the plant. Later they can cut tassels and bore into the cobs. (Source: KALRO; Wikipedia)

Fall armyworm scouting: check the field every week. A simple method is to walk across the field in a W shape and check 5 groups of 10 plants each, counting plants with fresh damage or droppings. (Source: Pacific Pests, Pathogens & Weeds)

Fall armyworm control without chemicals: plant early and at the same time as your neighbours, avoiding staggered planting. On small plots, crush egg masses and pick off caterpillars by hand. Putting sand, ash or soil into the whorl is a traditional method. Intercropping and push-pull (Desmodium between the rows, Napier or Brachiaria grass around the field) reduce attacks. Protect natural enemies such as small wasps, ants and birds. (Source: Pacific Pests, Pathogens & Weeds; Wikipedia)

Fall armyworm and pesticides: biological products such as Bt, Beauveria bassiana and fall armyworm virus (NPV), and plant extracts such as neem, can be used on young caterpillars. The pest arrived in Africa already resistant to many older insecticides, and chemical spraying is rarely profitable for smallholders and kills natural enemies. Ask your extension officer before spraying. (Source: Pacific Pests, Pathogens & Weeds)
