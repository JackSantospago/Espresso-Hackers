Crop rotation and intercropping: growing crops in sequence and together.

Crop rotation: growing a different crop on a field each season breaks the life cycle of pests and diseases that live on one crop family and survive in its residue. It also balances nutrient use and helps control weeds. (Source: Wikipedia)

Crop rotation examples: maize after beans benefits from the nitrogen the beans leave behind, and has less gray leaf spot and northern leaf blight than maize after maize. Beans should not follow beans or other legumes for 2 to 3 years, to reduce angular leaf spot, anthracnose and root rots. (Source: Crop Protection Network; Infonet-Biovision; Wikipedia)

Green manure and cover crops: legumes grown and then cut and left on or worked into the soil add nitrogen; grasses add organic matter and protect the soil from erosion and weeds. (Source: Wikipedia)

Intercropping: growing two or more crops in the same field at the same time, for example maize with beans or cowpeas. In low-input farming, intercropping can give higher or more stable total yields, spreads risk if one crop fails, and legumes add nitrogen. Bean rust can be lower when beans are intercropped with cereals. (Source: Wikipedia; Infonet-Biovision)

Intercropping tips: combine crops that do not compete too much, for example a tall cereal with a low legume, or a deep-rooted crop with a shallow-rooted one. Relay cropping plants the second crop while the first is still growing. (Source: Wikipedia)
